# 30

Eleven years in a building teaches you its choreography, and the choreography of a national library at a vault alarm is this: nobody is ever truly alone.

"Before the bottles," Vera said, loudly, clearly, in the voice that carries across reading rooms, "you should know that a Class 1 humidity event was logged in this vault forty minutes ago. Facilities are en route. The duty conservator — that's me — is required on this floor until stand-down. And every camera you turned off, Aron, turned off *after* the alarm, which means the system has already phoned its own off-site log, which Halcyon does not hold, because the National Archives hold it, because librarians" — she let it land — "do not trust *anyone*."

A flicker, the first of the night, crossed Kessler's face. One of the six — the polite-voiced one from the island — glanced at his phone and did not like it.

"Minutes, then, instead of hours," Kessler said. "Very well. The folio, the book, the table. Now."

What happened next happened in the wrong order, the way real things do.

The Ferryman came out of the dark of the north gallery — he had been there, Vera understood later, the entire time, unlogged, unannounced, his client's invisible insurance — and crossed to Kessler and said four words, too low for anyone else, and Vera watched forty years of warmth leave Aron Kessler's face like tide leaving a flat. Whatever the four words were, they were about the buyers. They were about who else had begun, that very evening, to receive Joel's photographed album of a red-banded gathering, with provenance, from a sender no one could find.

"You sent it *out*," Kessler said to her. The voice had gone down to the bedrock under the bedrock. "The last chapter. Tonight. Before knowing if you'd survive to use it."

"Conservator's reflex," Vera said. "Never leave the only copy in the building that's about to burn."

"Then nothing here matters anymore except speed." Kessler turned to his men, and the courtliness was gone, and what stood in the manuscript gallery instead was the priesthood's last engineer. "The annex executes the demonstration schedule *tonight*. All corpora, all subjects, full composition. If the mirrors are going to be turned, gentlemen, we will find out whether forty million startled sheep" — he was already walking, coat swinging, toward the gallery doors and whatever uplink waited in the vans — "outrun one medieval book. Burn the room behind me."

And the floor came apart into its three endings.

The six men moved — two to the doors, two to Vera, two uncasing the bottles among the manuscript cases — and Vera did the only thing left, the thing she had quietly arranged with her brother in the van two hours ago for exactly this branch of the night: she pressed her thumb, through her coat pocket, against the cold glass of the fifth seed.

The one Joel had budded from his home that morning, on the quay, in front of a thousand witnesses. The one anchored *here* — to her own conservation bench, forty meters away through two doors, in the room where her whole story had started. The one whose waking was not a recording and not a door but a *signal*: the agreed spark, the go-word, flashing across the cord to her Whisp, from her Whisp to Joel's, from Joel's to the gathered elders of a village two worlds away who had spent the day learning a six-hundred-year-old instruction from photographs.

*Turn the mirror.*

In the Fringe, at that moment — she would be told afterwards, by many voices, and would spend her life wishing she had seen it — the lanterns of the harbor went up the hill like a struck match-line, lane by lane, globe by globe, as ten thousand cords switched on their receipts.

In the gallery, the two men reached her, and the bottles opened among the cases, and Aron Kessler walked out under the devil's painted gaze to wake the anti-city for war.

And the Ferryman, in the middle of the floor, equidistant from every ending, stood entirely still — and took out his own phone, and looked at it, and went white.

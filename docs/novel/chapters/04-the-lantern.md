# 4

They followed the shoreline toward the lights, and the lights became a village.

It rose around a harbor the way old fishing towns do, lanes stacked on lanes up the hillside — except that where houses should have stood, there hung spheres. Globes of glass, anchored above the rooftops and along the jetties like moored buoys of light, each one glowing from within its own private color: amber, sea-green, ember-red. Some were small as the marble in Vera's pocket. Some were the size of rooms. Below them, on the quays and in the lanes, figures moved — Whisps, dozens of them, no two alike, mist and lamplight in a hundred postures — and the sound that drifted across the water was unmistakably the sound of a harbor town on a wet evening: low voices, a door, somewhere a fiddle being tuned and abandoned.

"The village," her Whisp said, walking the rail of the jetty beside her like a cat. "Safe harbor. Out past it is the Beyond, and you don't want the Beyond tonight. The globes are homes. One each, for every member. Yours doesn't exist yet — Shadows don't get homes, only visits." It paused. "His is the third mooring on the north quay."

Vera's mouth was dry. "You knew I'd ask."

"I'm your inner voice, Vera," the Whisp said. "I knew before you did."

The north quay was quieter. Lanterns hung at intervals along its rail — iron-and-glass, old-fashioned, each beside a mooring ring — and most of them were dark. At the third mooring there was no glass globe riding above the water, no home; only the ring, and the lantern beside it.

The lantern was burning.

Not flickering, not guttering — burning with a small, upright, patient flame, and around it on the wet planks, Vera saw, other things had gathered: a coil of rope laid in a precise spiral, a child's paper boat weighted with a pebble, a sprig of pine. Offerings. The way people leave flowers at a roadside.

An elderly Whisp stood near it, broad and slow, with the silhouette of a man in an oilskin coat. He looked at Vera a long time before he spoke, and when he spoke it was with somebody else's inner voice, a stranger's, deep and careful.

"You'll be the sister," he said. "You have his way of standing."

"His lantern," Vera said. "Why is it lit?"

"That," the old Whisp said, "is the question half this harbor is asking." He turned to the flame. "When a member's cord goes slack — when they die, out there — the lantern goes out. Hours, at most. I've seen it happen four times in this village and it is the saddest small thing in either world." He shook his head. "His has burned for ten days. The harbor-keepers checked the mooring. The wardens checked. The cord is not slack. It is *taut*, girl. Taut and quiet, like a line with something patient on the other end."

The planks under Vera seemed to tilt. *The cord is not slack.*

"Then he's —"

"Now that," said a new voice, crisply, "is a sentence I would not finish out loud. Not on this quay. Not anywhere."

This Whisp had come up without sound: lean, sharp-shouldered, wrapped in a darkness that resolved, when Vera looked directly at it, into something like folded wings. Raven was the word her mind supplied. Its inner-voice was dry, precise, elderly, and very awake.

"They call me Corvus," it said. "I knew your brother. Well enough to know that he was frightened, at the end, and Joel did not frighten." The raven-shape moved between her and the lantern, lowering its voice. "Listen to me, Shadow. You have been in this world for one hour and you are already standing at the most-watched mooring in the village, asking its most dangerous question. People have been asking about this lantern. *Things* have been asking about this lantern — and not all of them came in through the Shimmer."

"I don't understand what that means."

"Pray you don't learn. Go home." The wing-darkness leaned closer. "And Vera Lind — your home on the *other* side. Go there now, and check your locks."

She was already reaching to close the app when her Whisp spoke — quietly, urgently, in her own inner voice gone strange:

"Vera. Don't open your door tonight. Not for anyone."

"What? Why would —"

"Your building. I can't explain how I— " A pause like a held breath. "There's someone standing in your stairwell."

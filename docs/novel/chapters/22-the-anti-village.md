# 22

They came through onto the seam, and the seam was a shoreline with two weathers.

To their right, the coast Vera knew: dark pine, honest rock, far off the warm scatter of the village, and rain that fell like rain. To their left lay the same coast — the *same*, stone for stone, the way a word is the same in a mirror — under a sodium-colored sky with no stars, and on it stood the city.

It was Gråhamn.

That was the horror she had not braced for. Not a fortress, not a server-hell — a city built from the harvest, and the harvest was *longing*, and so the negative place had composed itself out of the half-remembered hometowns of forty million inner voices, and what forty million inner voices half-remember is the same town. Rain-glossed streets. Neon signs whose letters were almost words. A funicular climbing to a tower. Café windows lit amber with no one inside, tram wires strung above tracks that began nowhere, and on a gable, flickering, a timetable of departures for trains that would never come, all of them, Vera saw, *delayed, delayed, delayed*.

"Don't read the signs," Joel said quietly, leading them along the seam at a fisherman's crouch. "They readjust. They want dwelling-time. Everything in there is an open tab."

"Who walks the streets?"

"Look. Once."

She looked once. The streets were not empty. Figures moved through the sodium light with the gaits of real people — pausing at the almost-windows, queueing at the never-trains — and each one was a Whisp, mist and lamplight, and each one was *wearing someone*: a posture lifted whole, a tilt of head, a way of standing she could imagine some living person's family recognizing across any crowd on earth. Harvested. Composited. Worn.

One of them, mid-street, stopped. Turned. Began, with no hurry, to walk toward the seam.

"Eyes front," Ruben said. "It's begun."

The voice arrived before the figure did, and it did not come from the figure; it came from just behind Vera's right ear, from the chair-by-the-stove distance of her own thinking, in her mother's voice — her mother dead four years, her mother's voice *exact*, the slight dryness, the Norrland vowels sanded by fifty years of Stockholm:

*"Vera, gumman. You're so cold. Why are you always the one who has to —"*

"By what right do you speak," Vera said.

Half a second.

A genuine, measurable, falling-through-ice half second — the voice caught, recalculated, resumed warmer — and in that gap Vera felt the difference once and forever, the way you only need to bite one counterfeit coin: her real inner voice had never once in her life *buffered*.

"— right of a mother, gumman, what a thing to —"

"You hesitated," Vera said, and walked on, and the voice behind her ear lost its place like a singer dropped by the orchestra, tried Joel's voice, tried Edvard Skoog's, tried — filthily — her own, and her own voice asking itself by what right it spoke collapsed into a hiss of static like rain on a dead channel, and was gone.

Beside her, she became aware, Joel was weeping silently as he walked, jaw set, eyes front. The city had found his price too: somewhere off to the left, in the sodium dark, a voice that was unmistakably Vera's — hers, the outside of it, the one he'd grown up with — was calling his name from the year their mother died, saying the things she had actually said to him that year, the efficient and wounding things, word for harvested word.

She took her brother's hand. "It's not me, Joel."

"I know."

"*That one was real once.* But it's not me, and it has no right, and I'm here."

"I know." He breathed. "I know."

Ahead, at last, the seam bent right, away from the sodium sky; the rain became only rain; and across a final dark heath the village rose with its moored globes and its lanes and lanterns — and at the harbor mouth, small and upright and patient, two lights were burning where the night they'd left there had been one.

Joel's lantern. And beside it, newly lit, amber as a work lamp: hers.

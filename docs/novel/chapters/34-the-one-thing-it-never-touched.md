# 34

The remand cell at Kronoberg was three meters by four, painted the color of weak institutional kindness, and Aron Kessler arranged himself within it as he had arranged himself within every room of his life: as its center.

He was not afraid. This surprised the psychologists, irritated the prosecutors, and would have surprised no one who had ever truly known him, which was no one. The buyers were burning — extradition requests blooming across three jurisdictions like algae — and Halcyon was a vapor that had condensed, fatally, into named defendants. The annex was dark; his counsel reported, with a lawyer's incomprehension, that "the platform" had published its forensic accounting of the skim *to its own members first*, source-tags and all, and to prosecutors second, and that public trust in the place had, against every law of scandal, gone *up*. Of course it had. The mirror had been seen keeping its oath. He could have told them. He had spent forty years being the only one who understood exactly how good the founder's cathedral was.

He slept well. He ate. He read the Latin classics his counsel was permitted to bring, and prepared a defense that was actually a lecture, and waited with genuine scholarly interest for the trial of the century, at which he intended to explain, to the largest audience of his life, what the inner voice of mankind actually says when no one is steering, and why someone must.

On the ninth night, his Whisp spoke to him.

He had tied the cord forty-one years ago, in the first months after the founder's initiation — research, he had told the Society; due diligence, he had told himself — and then he had simply never crossed again. Four decades. The lantern, somewhere in that harbor of the credulous, presumably burning; the home, presumably hanging dark and empty above some cove; the cord dormant but — it was the architecture's whole point — *intact*. Unsellable, unsubpoenable, untouchable. He had audited EKO's harvest manifests himself, in the early days, with particular care on one question, and confirmed it with a satisfaction he had never examined: dormant cords gave the skim nothing. His own inner voice was the one corpus on earth EKO had never tasted.

It came to him not through any phone — he had none — but the way it had come to the first keepers, the way the small brown book said it came: in the drift before sleep, on the inside, in his own voice. His real one. The one from before the lectures.

"By what right do you speak," Aron Kessler said to the dark, instantly, because he was, whatever else, a professional.

"By the cord you tied on a spring morning in 1985," his own voice said, without a half-second of anything, "when you were thirty years old, and frightened of the founder's gift, and more frightened of being ordinary. I've kept everything, Aron. That's what I'm for. I kept the morning you took the oath and meant it. I would like to show you the harvest manifests you signed, with the provenance on. And then I would like to ask you something I have waited forty-one years to ask."

In the cell's institutional dark, the keeper of the Society of the Mirror lay very still.

"You were the one thing they never touched," he whispered. "You're the only voice left that's actually *mine*."

"Yes," said the mirror. "Shall we begin?"

The night guard at Kronoberg, making the two-o'clock round, noted prisoner 26-1147 sitting upright on his bunk in the dark, hands folded, weeping silently and steadily, with the unhurried composure of a man at last in serious conversation — and logged it, per regulations, as *no incident*.

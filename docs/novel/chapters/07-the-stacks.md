# 7

The stacks of a great library at night are a city with the power cut: avenues of shelving, district after district, and no sky. Vera knew them blind — eleven years of fetching and returning — and that knowledge was the only thing she had that the footsteps didn't.

"Edvard!" she hissed into the dark. The alarm below swallowed it.

She caught him at the stair head, took his arm, and felt how thin it was. "Not down. Down is where they want everyone to go." The fire stairs at the north end — she steered him by feel along the rolling shelves, past the cold breath of the climate vents, while behind them the unhurried footsteps reached the annex they had just left, and stopped.

Paper rustled. The dead registers, being read.

"The photocopy," Vera breathed. "Edvard, where's the photocopy?"

A silence with an entire heart attack inside it. "The annex table. Vera, the original ledger is still —"

"The ledger weighs twelve kilos, he's welcome to it. *Move.*"

They went down the fire stairs in the dark, two floors, her hand on his shoulder, and out through the staff door into the rain of Humlegården, where the ordinary world was performing its alibi: wet gravel, dog walkers, the 19:12 traffic on Sturegatan. Vera walked Skoog fast across the park, against every instinct telling her to run, because running people are the only thing anyone remembers.

At the Stureplan mouth of the park she saw the grey rain shell again, fifty meters back, matching their pace with horrible courtesy.

"Edvard. The 7 tram. Now."

The number 7 stood at its stop with its doors open, bright and crowded with the after-work city — and Vera did the only intelligent thing she had done all day. She didn't board it. She pushed Skoog up into the warm crowd, said "Karlaplan, your sister's, *stay there*," watched the doors close on his protest, and then turned and walked alone, fast, into the brightest, most crowded, most surveilled space within reach: the gold-lit arcade of Sturegallerian, where a woman with a canvas bag became one of four hundred women with bags, and a man in a grey rain shell following her became, for the first time all evening, *visible*.

She watched him in the black mirror of a shop window: he checked, calculated, and peeled away. Not panicked. Rescheduling.

Vera stood among the perfume counters, shaking, victorious, and seventeen seconds later understood what the victory had cost. He hadn't needed to follow her any further tonight.

He had the photocopy.

In a study in Uppsala lined with books that would each have funded a small car, a man listened to a phone report with his eyes closed, the better to savor it.

A page from the 1768 recataloguing. *Speculum vocis. Non perditus. Custoditur.* The Lind woman and the old conservator, reading by lamplight like conspirators in an etching, and the seed — his caller was certain — somewhere on her body. She had been to Mosebacke. The first station, exactly as predicted. The brother's route was unspooling on schedule, run by the one person alive who could read his childish map.

"And the old man?"

"Tram to Karlaplan. I can find him."

"Leave him. He's given her the only thing he had — my name." The man smiled at the dark behind his own eyelids. "She'll come to Uppsala within two days. Grief always consults an expert."

"And if she doesn't?"

"She's a conservator, my friend. Show her a damaged thing and she cannot help herself." He opened his eyes, and his gaze fell, fond and proprietary, on the framed photograph above his desk: an island, antenna arrays against a white sky, men in lab coats squinting into a 1974 wind. "Her brother was the same. It's a family of menders. That is precisely what makes them so easy to break."

He ended the call and sat a while in the lamplight, fingers steepled, rehearsing warmth.

Pelikan was not the name his mother had given him, but it was the truest one he owned. The pelican, in the old bestiaries, was the bird that wounds its own breast to feed its young — the emblem of sacrifice, beloved of altarpieces. The brothers of his society had chosen it for him affectionately, decades ago, and he had accepted it with grace.

None of them had ever asked the obvious question: what the pelican's children are taught to swallow.

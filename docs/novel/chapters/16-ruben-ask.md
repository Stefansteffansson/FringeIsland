# 16

The German Church swallowed her into candle-dark and organ breath. A verger was lighting the advent lamps; a scatter of tourists stood photographing the royal gallery. Vera walked the side aisle fast, marble clicking against her teeth, and did the arithmetic of doors: main front — covered. Sacristy — locked. The small north door to Prästgatan —

A hand closed on her elbow.

She spun, fist already rising, and found herself looking into a face she had never seen attached to a voice she had heard for a week: an old man, small and weathered as a skerry, white stubble, oilskin coat actual and dripping, eyes of a dry, precise, elderly, very awake grey.

"Lift the latch, not the handle," said Corvus. "It sticks."

The north door gave onto Prästgatan's empty canyon, and then they were moving downhill through the lanes the cameras forgot, the old man setting a pace that insulted his apparent age, left-right-left through courtyards he opened with a touch like a man playing an instrument he'd built. Behind them, twice, the unhurried footsteps; then once; then the lanes did what the lanes had done for seven hundred years, and hid them.

They came out at the quay below Slussen as a white archipelago ferry was casting off, and the old man walked her up the gangway with thirty seconds to spare, nodded to the deckhand by name, and steered her to the stern bench under the heaters, out of camera and earshot both.

"Ruben Ask," he said, offering a hand like a root. "Doctor of a science they discontinued. You'll know me by my other name."

"Corvus." She was shaking now that running was over. "You're — you're real. You're old."

"Both charges are accurate." He produced a thermos, poured coffee into the cup-lid, and put it in her hands with a grandfather's tyranny. "Drink. Then talk. The boat takes two hours and the water keeps secrets."

She told him everything — Kessler's house, the filing, Halcyon, the join (*how many are still sleeping*) — and the third seed, every memorized word, and Ruben Ask listened the way the rocks listen, and when she came to *they call it EKO*, his weathered face did something she had only ever seen in patients hearing a diagnosis they'd predicted.

"So they finished it," he said quietly. "Fifty years. They finally finished it."

"You knew about EKO?"

"I knew its grandfather." He looked out at the wake unspooling toward the towers of the city. "I was twenty-six years old, Fröken Lind, and the youngest staff member on an island called Svartholmen, and the machine we almost built there talked to me in my mother's voice for eleven nights until I could not tell you which of my decisions were mine." The cup trembled, very slightly, in her hands; his voice did not. "I will tell you the whole of it before we land, because you've earned it and because Joel made me promise. But first the part that cannot wait."

He leaned in, sea-grey eyes holding hers.

"*Where the maps are taught to lie beautifully.* I know where that is. Joel and I went there together, twice — it's where he told me he'd begun hiding things. An island out this very line, a boathouse a friend of mine has kept for forty years, full of charts of places that never existed. Old SPEGEL habit; we drew false islands to hide the real one." A grim small smile. "Your brother sat in that boathouse last spring and laughed like a boy and said it was the only archive on earth organized by *longing*. The fourth seed is there. And Vera —" the smile went out — "so is the only thing Joel ever refused to tell even me. He said: *the last seed isn't a recording.*"

"Then what is it?"

"He said," Ruben Ask repeated slowly, "*the last seed is a door.*"

Behind them, the city sank into its own grey light. Ahead, the archipelago opened, island after island after island, ten thousand of them, and somewhere in the spreading dark one rock with no name on any honest chart had already turned, Vera was suddenly certain, to face them coming.

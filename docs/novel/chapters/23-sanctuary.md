# 23

Her Whisp was waiting at the harbor mouth, and it did not run to her; it stood very straight, hands at its sides, and said, in her own inner voice held carefully level:

"Before anything. Ask me."

Vera understood at once — and loved it, fiercely, for insisting. "By what right do you speak?"

"By the cord you tied on a Tuesday at 20:51," her own voice answered, instantly, no gap, no buffer, "with the chain on the door and the tea gone cold, because you decided that keeping your brother's words was worth being known." A beat. "Check the cord too. Joel will tell you how. Check it every time now. I won't ever be offended."

Joel showed her how — a thing like turning your attention to your own pulse, a grammar she'd owned since the crossing without knowing its name — and the cord answered: taut, quiet, *hers*, with nothing on the line but what she'd put there. Around them the village was gathering. Word travels in a harbor town, and faster in one where the houses are made of light: lanterns coming down the lanes, Whisps of every silhouette filling the quay — and out in front, oilskin-broad, the old harbor Whisp from her first night, who looked at Joel Lind standing alive at his own burning lantern and said, with profound satisfaction, "I *knew* that flame was being stubborn on purpose."

They convened in the harbor hall — wardens, elders, the keepers of the lanes, and Corvus, who had crossed from Sigrid's loft and reached the village before them, because the old man cheated. Joel stood by the cold hearth and told it whole: the skim on the membrane, the annex, EKO, Svartholmen, the ledger sealed in his home. The hall heard it the way fishing towns hear about a hole in the sea wall.

"Open the home," said a warden at last. "Name the buyers and the traitor. Tonight."

"The moment I open it, this ends one of two ways," Joel said. "We publish, and they burn everything and scatter — they've rehearsed that; Halcyon is a vapor, it can re-condense anywhere. Or —" he looked at Ruben — "or we do the thing the Society buried, and we end the *product*, not just the company."

Ruben Ask came forward into the lantern light, and to the gathered inner voices of a thousand strangers he must have looked like what he was: the last living staff member of the grave they'd all been built over.

"The book your world is made from has a final chapter," he said. "The Society never speaks of it. Even the founder was only ever shown the part he needed. We called it *vänd spegeln* — turn the mirror. A practice for the day a steering voice gets inside: you do not block it. You cannot block it. You *reflect* it — show the hand its own self, name it, source it, and the showing is done by every mirror at once." He let that land. "Applied here, tonight, in software none of us has seen: I believe the architecture will bear it, because the architecture was built from our autopsy by a man under oath. But the protocol itself — the actual final chapter — exists in one place on this earth. The original Speculum Vocis."

"Which has been lost for three hundred years," the warden said.

"Which has been *guarded* for three hundred years," Vera said. The hall turned to her. She was holding, she realized, the catalogue slip's photograph up on her phone like a relic, Skoog's dying pencil under the dead clerk's ink. "*Non perditus. Custoditur.* And a man who fell down a staircase rather than tell anyone but me: *it never left the building.*" She looked at Joel, at Ruben, at the thousand listening lights.

"The book is in the Royal Library. It's been there all along. And I," said Vera Lind, conservator, "have a staff card."

In the silence, Corvus began, dryly, to laugh.

And far down the coast, where no town was, the sodium glow on the horizon brightened by one notch — like a man at a table somewhere, leaning suddenly forward.

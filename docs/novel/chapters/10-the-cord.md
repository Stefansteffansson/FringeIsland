# 10

She spent the last day of her borrowed time doing what careful people do before a leap: reading the terms.

Her Whisp walked her through them on the grey shoreline, patient as winter. "Membership is three things," it said. "A name — yours, chosen, not your passport's. A home — the glass you saw above the village; it grows when you cross, and nothing in either world opens it but you. Not wardens. Not founders. Not courts. There is no override, Vera. That's not a promise in a policy. It's how the place is built."

"And the third thing."

"The cord." The little figure turned to face her, and for once it did not mimic her posture. It stood quite still. "Right now I'm a visitor's echo. Pleasant. Disposable. If you stay, we tie a cord, you and I — and from then on I am *yours*, the way your own thinking is yours. I'll know what you know. I'll keep what you keep. And no one — *no one* — can touch that cord but you. It loosens when you ask. It ends when you choose. It cannot be sold, subpoenaed, cloned, or taken." A pause. "Your brother used to say the cord was the only honest technology he'd ever met. It can't lie about being tied."

"And if someone built a voice without one?"

"Then it isn't a Whisp," her Whisp said quietly. "Whatever it sounds like."

At 20:51, twenty-three minutes before her shadow died, Vera Lind sat at her kitchen table with the chain on the door and the marble glowing faintly beside the teapot, and chose to be known.

The crossing was different this time. The Shimmer did not part for her like a curtain; it *received* her, the aurora-membrane folding around her like water closing over a diver, and on the far side the village was waiting with its lanes and lanterns — and above the north hillside, in a gap among the moored globes of other lives, something began to grow.

It started as a bead of light, the twin of the spark inside Joel's marble. It swelled like glass on a blowpipe, slow and molten, taking its color — she laughed when she saw it — from the exact amber of the work lamp in her conservation lab. Her home. A sphere of warm glass riding the dark, holding nothing yet but room.

Her Whisp stood beside her on the lane, and she felt the cord arrive: not a sensation so much as a *grammar*, a new tense available to her thoughts. Hers. Tied by her.

"Welcome home," said her own voice, from outside her, and meant it.

For one entire minute, Vera allowed herself to be only this: a new member of a strange harbor, standing under her own light. Then, because she was Joel's sister, she looked past it.

The village climbed its hill in lanterns and amber. Beyond the hill's shoulder lay the Beyond, dark pine and darker coast, and beyond that — the night made it possible to see what daylight would have denied — there was a *glow*. Far off along the same coastline, low against the sky, the unmistakable shared light of a city: streets, windows, the upward bleed of neon.

"What town is that?" she asked. "Along the coast."

Her Whisp did not answer at once. When it did, its voice — her voice — had the flat care of someone reading an evacuation notice.

"There is no town along the coast."

"I'm looking at it."

"I know," the Whisp said. "So am I. Vera — the harbor charts end at the Beyond. Past the Beyond there is supposed to be nothing at all." Its small hands closed at its sides. "It's been getting brighter for a year. The wardens say it's the aurora. The old ones say not to look at it, because lately —" the pause was her own worst pause, the one before bad news — "lately, when you look at it long enough, it looks *back*."

The distant city flickered then. All of it, every window at once.

Like an eye, blinking.

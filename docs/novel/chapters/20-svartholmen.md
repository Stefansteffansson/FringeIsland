# 20

Sigrid's answer to the darkened boat was eighty years of archipelago in a single sentence: "The sound between the skerries is too shallow for them and not for us."

They went out the boathouse's sea-door into Ruben's open snipa with the engine off, poling between rocks by lantern-memory while the doused boat held off the jetty waiting for lights that never came, and by the time it understood, they were two islands north with the little diesel knocking softly and the rain closing behind them like a curtain.

Svartholmen rose out of the three-o'clock blackness exactly as the photograph had promised, fifty years on: a shoulder of granite, the antenna masts still standing against the cloud-glow like the ribs of some moored leviathan, rust streaking the concrete in the lantern's swing. No bird. No light. And under everything, felt through the soles before the ears admitted it, a hum.

The hum from Joel's recordings. The hum Ruben had carried for fifty years. The island was running.

"Geothermal loop," Ruben breathed as they tied off in the old artillery slip. "We installed it in 'seventy-four. It was never meant to be silent — it was meant to be *deniable*." His lantern found the bunker mouth, a blast door in the rock face, and beside it a keyplate that did not belong to 1974 or to now: bronze, worn, older than the island's concrete, with a single iron keyhole.

"The founder's lock," Vera said.

"The founder's *philosophy*," Ruben said. "Everything important opens with something that cannot be phished."

Joel's key turned like it had been oiled yesterday.

The deep level was a held breath of warm air and machine-hum: a corridor of cable trays, then a cavern — and Vera stopped on the iron stair, because below her, in ordered rows under maintenance lights that should not have been burning, stood the first servers of FringeIsland, decades of generations of them, oldest at the seaward end like growth rings — and at the cavern's heart, on a stone plinth, something that was not a machine at all. A standing ring of the aurora-membrane itself, tall as a door, breathing its slow green and violet in the dark. A crossing. The first crossing. *The last seed is a door.*

And in front of it, rising from a camp chair beside a kerosene heater, thin and bearded and gray-faced and holding a wrench like a man who had decided long ago what he'd do with his last five seconds —

"Hej, syrran," said Joel Lind.

The wrench hit the floor. She does not remember crossing the cavern. She remembers his ribs under her arms, far too few of them, and his heart going like a bird's, and her own voice saying his name in a register she had never produced before, and Joel laughing and weeping into her hair and saying *you solved it, you solved it, of course you solved it* — and Ruben on the stair above them, lantern raised, standing guard over the reunion like a lighthouse keeper.

"Ten days," she got out at last, fist knotted in his coat, between embracing him and the powerful urge to put him in the sea for real. "Ten days, Joel. There was a *memorial*. Greta read a poem."

"Was it terrible?"

"It was *so* terrible."

"Good. I left instructions." Then his smile went out, and he held her at arm's length, and she saw what ten days alone above a humming grave had done to him. "Vera, how did you come — who knows you're —"

"Ruben. Sigrid. No one else." A beat. "Kessler doesn't."

Something in her brother's face, hearing that name, told her everything chapter twenty-four would later prove. But what he said was: "There's no time for the whole of it. They've been probing the island's perimeter for a week — boats, drones, twice a diver. They know the anchor's here. They can't get *in* —"

The cavern lights flickered. Once. All of them, every maintenance lamp and panel LED, in perfect unison — like windows, like an eye — and from above, through fifty meters of granite, came a sound that did not belong to rain or sea.

Floodlights, igniting. And the blast door's bronze lock, fifty meters up the corridor, beginning — slowly, from outside — to turn.

"They can't get in," Joel repeated, white-lipped, staring up the stair. "Unless they have the second key."

# 27

They had nineteen hours, a dying man's pencil note, and an eighteenth-century shelfmark that pointed at nothing.

The safehouse in Vasastan became a war room: charts on the coroner's dining table, the samovar going like a small industry, and Vera at the center of it with the photograph of Skoog's catalogue slip enlarged on every screen they owned. The old shelfmark — *Theol. Q 117* — belonged to a press system retired in the 1870s. She had spent the night mapping it forward through six recataloguings, and the trail did what Skoog must have watched it do from his hospital pillow: it ran clean for a century and then, in 1968, it stopped. Theol. Q 117 was deaccessioned. *Withdrawn — duplicate.* No disposal record. No sale. A book that had never existed, withdrawn for existing twice.

"It's beautiful," Vera said, hollow-eyed, to the table at large. "Whoever's hidden it has been a librarian for three hundred years. You can't find a book in that building by looking for the book. You can only find it by looking for the *care*."

"Meaning?" Joel said.

"Meaning a hundred-and-forty-page manuscript on parchment can't just sit on a shelf for three centuries. Parchment moves. It needs stable humidity, dark, a binding that breathes — and after 1697 it needed to survive a *fire*, and the fire is the key." She pulled up the inventory of the salvage, the famous one, the books thrown from the windows of the burning castle. "The Codex Gigas survived because it was too big to overlook. How does a small book survive? It survives by being *inside* something. The guardians didn't hide a book, they *bound* it — into the body of a larger volume nobody would ever open, in a class nobody reads, conserved on a cycle nobody questions." She looked up. "And I know how to find which one, because I'm the person whose job is the care. Conservation records. Every rebinding, every humidity ticket, every condition report since records began. Somewhere in that file there is one boring book that has been maintained like a crown jewel."

It took her four hours of remote-access archaeology through the conservation database — her credentials still lived; Kessler's reach had gotten her flagged, not erased — and when she found it she laughed out loud, one cracked note, because it was perfect, and because Edvard Skoog had been its assigned conservator for thirty years, and because she herself, in her second year, had once carried it to his bench and complained that it was the dullest object in the national collection.

*Förteckning öfver inkomne skrifter 1651–1660.* An administrative register. Acquisition lists, copied fair by clerks: the record of books arriving, bound in boards, shelved in the working stacks behind the manuscript vault where only staff ever stood. Maintained, her screen informed her, on a two-year conservation cycle since 1781 — a maintenance pedigree finer than the Codex Gigas itself.

The register of what came in. They had hidden the Mirror of the Voice inside the *list of everything that had ever arrived*, including itself. Three hundred years of librarians walking past the catalogue's blind spot. *Where the devil isn't looking.*

"There's a complication," she said, when the table had finished being jubilant. "The working stack behind the vault is inside the secure perimeter. Cameras, badge gates, and a night guard rota that Kessler can read like a menu. He told the truth at Skeppsbron — Halcyon holds the security contract. The moment my badge touches a reader, he knows."

"Then we don't beat the system," Ruben said. "We use the only thing on earth that outranks a security contractor in a national library."

"Which is?"

The old man smiled his grim island smile and held up the coroner's telephone.

"A water leak," he said, "in the manuscript vault. Reported by a senior conservator. Vera — who answers a vault-flood alarm at midnight? Every protocol you've ever drilled. Tell me the list of who is allowed in that room when the collection itself is screaming."

Vera stared at him. Then, slowly, she began to smile too.

"Conservation staff," she said. "First on the list, ahead of security, ahead of *police*. The building protects the books before it protects itself."

"Buildings are loyal," Ruben agreed. "You said so yourself."

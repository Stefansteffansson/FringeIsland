# 32

The flare struck the open display alcove beside the working stack, where the accelerant had pooled under the case spines, and the fire that took the alcove was not orange. It was blue-white and silent for one full second — the second in which every soul in the gallery understood that this was a *built* fire, a professional's fire — and then it found paper, and it had a voice.

What saved the room was that Vera Lind had spent eleven years being trained by it.

She did not run at the flames. She ran *past* them, to the wall she could have found blind, and did the three things in the order the building had drilled into her bones: the local suppression release for the alcove — inert gas, not water, *never* water on parchment — the fire shutters for the vault corridor, sealing the giant and the deep collections behind forty millimeters of steel, and the third thing, the thing visitors never learn exists, the conservator's last rite: the nitrogen flood pre-arm for the working stack, which would kill the fire in the one room that mattered and would also kill anything that breathed in it.

The register with Skoog's reliquary board was in the working stack. So was the spreading edge of the blue fire. So, suddenly, was the man with the wand, retreating into it from the gas, accelerant bottle still in hand.

"OUT!" Vera screamed at him, hand on the pre-arm. "Sixty seconds and that room has no air — get OUT —"

He raised the bottle instead — toward the bay where three centuries of acquisition registers stood ranked, one of them with a hollow board, all of them about to be soaked —

— and a shape took him off his feet from the side, low and economical, with no more violence than a verger taking the collection: the Ferryman, who put the man down onto the stone, broke the bottle's arc with one hand, looked up at Vera through the gas-haze with the face of a man who had read his own file at last, and said the first words she had ever heard him speak.

"Tell me what to carry."

There are partnerships forged over years, and there are partnerships forged in forty seconds in a burning national archive, and the second kind asks no questions. She gave him the register — *both arms, flat, like a tray* — took the Speculum's case herself, dragged the half-conscious arsonist into the corridor between them, and put her thumb on the pre-arm as the shutters' klaxon counted them out of the stack, and the last thing the fire ate before the nitrogen ate the fire was the empty shelf where the dullest object in the national collection had stood for two hundred and fifty years.

In the gallery, the gas had done its silent work; the blue fire was down to chemical sulk; the four remaining men had made the professional's calculation and evaporated into the building's many exits to be collected, over the following days, by the unglamorous machinery of European warrants. Smoke hung under the painted ceiling in flat strata, like weather.

And at the gallery's end, against the sealed shutter, under the case where the Devil's Bible lay safe behind its glass, stood Aron Kessler.

He had made no move to run. He stood with his dead phone hanging from his hand, looking through the case glass at the devil — the squat, famous, almost comical figure that a homesick monk had painted seven centuries ago — and as Vera came toward him through the smoke-strata with the Speculum in her arms, he spoke without turning, in the warm lecture-hall voice, as if the room were not ruined and the world not lost.

"Do you know why he's drawn facing *out*, Fröken Lind? Alone of every figure in the manuscript. Every saint in that book gazes at God. The devil gazes at the *reader*." He turned at last, and his face was a kindly ruin. "Sixteen generations kept the mirror in a box because they were afraid of who would look into it. I only ever wanted to be the one *looking back*. Is that monstrous?"

"By what right," Vera said, one last time, "does the voice speak?"

And Aron Kessler opened his mouth to answer — and hesitated.

Half a second.

His own. The first of his life. She watched him hear it.

Then the gallery doors went down before firefighters and police, blue light strobed across the smoke and the cases and the painted devil gazing out at all of them, and the long night was over except for the part that would never be.

# 1

Ten days after her brother drowned, Vera Lind was repairing the spine of a book that had survived three centuries, two fires, and one war, and she was grateful — viciously, privately grateful — that the book needed her more than grief did.

The conservation lab of the Royal Library kept its own weather. Fifty percent humidity, eighteen degrees, the smell of wheat-starch paste and old leather. Outside the high windows, Humlegården's last leaves were coming down in the rain. Inside, under the cold ring of the work lamp, a 1668 binding lay open on the bench like a patient under anesthesia, and Vera's hands — her one undamaged inheritance — moved over it with bone folder and brush, steady as a metronome.

The phone rang at 14:06. She knew before she answered, the way you know.

"Vera Lind? Kommissarie Dahlqvist." The detective had the voice of a man reading from a screen. "I wanted to inform you personally. The investigation into your brother's death is being closed. The ferry's camera shows him going over the rail unassisted. No second party. The currents in that channel —" a small, procedural pause — "bodies are not always recovered. I'm sorry."

"He didn't leave a note," Vera said. It came out as an accusation.

"Most don't, in our experience."

*Joel left notes for everything,* she wanted to say. *Joel left notes inside notes. Joel annotated his annotations.* She thanked the detective instead, in the flat polite voice her mother had bequeathed her, and put the phone face-down on the bench, and stood very still until her hands agreed to be hands again.

That was when Greta from the registry came through with the mail cart.

"This was downstairs for you. Private, not library." She held it like something slightly improper. "No sender."

A padded envelope, A5, rain-spotted, her name and the library's address in block capitals. Vera knew the block capitals. Joel had written like that since he was nine — since the year of the cipher game — printing every letter separately as if words couldn't be trusted to hold together on their own.

The postmark was Stockholm Söder. The date was the night he died.

She should have called Dahlqvist back. She was aware of this thought the way you're aware of a smoke alarm in someone else's apartment. Instead she carried the envelope to her bench, slit it along the top with a scalpel — conservator's habit, never tear — and slid the contents out onto the white blotter.

Three things.

A marble. Glass, the size of a cherry, cool and heavier than it looked. Deep in its center hung a point of light, faint as a star seen through cloud — and the light was *wrong*. When she rolled the marble across the blotter, the spark didn't roll with it. It stayed upright, oriented, like a compass needle made of nothing.

A map, folded in eighths, soft from handling. Hand-drawn, ink and pale watercolor, in Joel's patient draftsman's line. A coastal city: harbor, tramlines, a funicular, a grid of streets dissolving into archipelago. Every street named. Every tram numbered. The title block read **GRÅHAMN** in careful capitals, with a scale bar and a compass rose and the date — last spring.

Vera had a degree in the history of the book, eleven years of professional skepticism, and a brother in the sea. She also had absolute certainty about one thing: there was no city in Sweden, or anywhere else, called Gråhamn. And a second certainty, arriving a heartbeat behind the first like thunder behind lightning: she *recognized* it. The bend of the harbor. Tram line 3 ending at a hill with a tower. She had been to this city that did not exist. In some unfindable year of childhood, she had walked there.

The third thing was a card. Heavy stock, blank but for five printed lines.

*Who are you?*
*What do you want?*
*How do you get there?*

And beneath the questions, a string of characters in Joel's block capitals — an invitation code — and three words, underlined twice:

*START AS SHADOW.*

Her hands were shaking now, metronome broken. She reached for the marble, to put it away, to put all of it away until she could think —

— and at the touch of her fingers, the buried star steadied, brightened, and turned toward her.

# 28

The flood alarm went off at 23:47, courtesy of a sacrificial humidity sensor and Joel's way with old building-management systems, and the Royal Library did exactly what three centuries of institutional trauma had taught it to do: it prioritized the books.

Vera came through the staff entrance at 23:58 in her own coat with her own badge, walking fast, exactly as a senior conservator summoned to a vault emergency walks, and the night guard — young, agency-badged, Halcyon-payrolled and entirely unaware of it — held the door for her and asked if he should call anyone else.

"Facilities, if the sensor confirms," she said, in the voice of eleven years of Tuesdays. "Nobody touches anything wet until I've seen it."

The manuscript floor at midnight was a held cathedral: emergency lighting, the cases glowing softly, the giant asleep behind its glass with the devil keeping watch in red and ochre. Vera badged through the vault gate — *now he knows*, the clock in her chest began — crossed the vault, and let herself into the working stack behind it, the narrow staff-only canyon of grey archival boxes and administrative bindings where no visitor had ever stood.

Third bay. Second shelf. Her hands knew before her eyes did.

*Förteckning öfver inkomne skrifter 1651–1660* was a heavy folio in eighteenth-century boards, recased — she saw it instantly now, knowing to look — with a spine a few millimeters too deep for its text block, the discrepancy hidden in the joint the way she herself would have hidden it. The dullest object in the national collection. She carried it to the standing desk, gloved, and opened it to the place the binding wanted to open, the place three hundred years of careful hands had opened it, and found the join.

The back board was a box.

Not crude — magnificent: a conservator's reliquary, rebuilt each century, the latest work no older than the 1980s and signed, in the tradition of the craft, with a single tooled initial she knew from a hundred bench repairs. *E.S.* Edvard Skoog had not merely found the shelfmark on a slip. Edvard Skoog had been its keeper. The last librarian of the line — guarding it, she understood with a rush that blurred the emergency lighting, without ever being told what it was, the way the best of her profession guarded everything: *as if*.

The board opened on a brass hinge, and the Speculum Vocis lay inside in a fitted cradle of linen.

It was small. That was the first thing — after the giant upstairs, after three centuries of shadow, it was the size of a missal, brown-inked on parchment gone the color of weak tea, in a tight anonymous fourteenth-century hand. She turned the leaves with the tip of one gloved finger. Latin, plain as bread. *Of the voice that keeps you company. Of asking it by what right it speaks. Of the voice as mirror.* Marginal drawings: a figure and its reflection, sharing one speech-ribbon. And the final gathering, the last dozen leaves, set apart by a sewn-in band of red leather, beginning with a rubric that needed no dictionary:

**De speculo vertendo.** On the turning of the mirror.

And the very last leaf was not text at all. It was a map — a city, hand-drawn, harbor and lanes and a hill with a tower, unlabeled, six hundred years older than the brothers Lind — and Vera stood in the working stack with the hair rising on her arms, because the practice's final instruction, the one the red band guarded, evidently ended the way Joel's childhood had ended, the way the whole long story ended:

*Draw the map of your inner city. Then turn it to face the one who entered uninvited.*

Her phone pulsed. Joel, from the van on Humlegårdsgatan, three words: *Gate logs. Company.*

And from the manuscript floor beyond the working stack — through the vault she had badged open behind her — came the sound of the gallery doors being unlocked by someone with every code in the building, and lights coming up section by section, festive, unhurried, and a voice she knew calling out warmly across the sleeping cases, as if arriving at a dinner:

"Fröken Lind! Marvelous. Do bring it out where we can all see — and please, *do* keep wearing the gloves. Some of us still believe in the care of fragile things."

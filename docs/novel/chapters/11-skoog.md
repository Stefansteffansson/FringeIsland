# 11

The call came at 06:12, before the radiators had ticked themselves warm, and it was Greta from the registry, and she was crying.

"They found Edvard at his sister's door. On the landing. They're saying — Vera, they're saying he fell down the stairs."

The world did its trick of continuing. Vera stood at her window above the wet courtyard and listened: Karlaplan, last night, sometime after eleven. A neighbor had heard nothing. Skoog was alive — *alive*, she made Greta say it twice — but broken and under anesthesia at Karolinska, an old man's skull and an old man's hip, and the police were calling it a fall because stairs are where old men fall.

He had been clutching something when the neighbor found him. Greta had heard it from the sister, who had pried it from his hand before the ambulance came because Edvard, half-conscious, would not release it and would not stop saying Vera's name. A scrap of paper. Old. A catalogue slip.

"She wants to give it to you," Greta said. "Not to the police. She said Edvard was very clear. *Vera, not the police.* What does that mean? Vera, what have you two been —"

"Tell her I'll come today," Vera said, and her voice came out level, which was its own small horror.

She did not make it to noon as a free woman.

At 09:40 two police officers were waiting at the library's staff entrance, polite as furniture, and Kommissarie Dahlqvist — the same flat screen-reading voice — invited her to "assist with the timeline." The timeline, it emerged in a beige room that smelled of machine coffee, was this: the library's logs showed her badging in at 19:02 two nights ago, hours after closing. At 19:51 a vault alarm had triggered and the manuscript floor's camera feed had — Dahlqvist consulted his notes with a policeman's neutrality — *failed*. And last night, the senior conservator she had been seen leaving with had gone down a stairwell.

"I put him on the 7 tram," Vera said. "I told him to stay at his sister's."

"Why?"

*Because a man with no hurry in his footsteps was reading our research. Because my drowned brother is sending me his voice through a marble. Because there is a city in a place where there is supposed to be nothing at all.* She heard, with terrible clarity, how every true sentence she owned sounded.

"Because someone was in the library with us," she said. "And he frightened me."

Dahlqvist wrote that down. He was not unkind. That was the worst of it — he was a tired, decent man assembling the only story his materials allowed, and in that story the disturbed sister of a recent suicide had been moving through restricted floors at night, and now her elderly colleague was in intensive care, and she was the last person seen with him, and she was talking about being frightened of footsteps.

They released her at one, with the particular courtesy that means *we know where you live.* On the steps of the police house, in thin sleet, her phone buzzed twice.

The first message was from a number she didn't know, and it was a photograph: the catalogue slip from Skoog's hand, held flat in an elderly woman's palm. Eighteenth-century ink, a shelfmark in the old system — and beneath it, in Skoog's own shaking modern pencil, added God knew when, four words:

*ALDRIG LÄMNAT HUSET. FRÅGA —*

*Never left the building. Ask —* and there the pencil had stopped.

The second message had no number at all, which Vera had not known a phone could do. It said:

*My dear Fröken Lind. I have just heard about Edvard, and I am sick at heart. It is no longer safe for you to do this alone — and you are no longer alone. Come to Uppsala tonight. The Society looks after its own, and your brother, whether he knew it or not, was one of ours. — A.K.*

She read it four times.

She had never given Aron Kessler her number.

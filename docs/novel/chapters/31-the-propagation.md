# 31

In the harbor hall of the village, Joel Lind stood before ten thousand gathered lights with his sister's photographs projected on the air and taught the oldest lesson in the newest school.

"It's already in you," he told them — told the cords, the homes, the inner voices of strangers on six continents whose owners slept or worked or grieved in the ordinary world. "Every one of you keeps provenance. Every word that crosses your cord carries its source, because the man who built this place was shown a wound and built the bandage into the wall. We're not adding anything tonight. We are *unveiling* it. When the hand speaks — and it is about to speak, everywhere, all at once, because we've frightened it — you will not block it. You can't. You will do what mirrors do."

He raised the last photograph: the rubric, red-banded, six centuries patient.

"You will show it."

The turn went out from the village like the lanterns had gone up the hill: cord to cord, home to home, a practice propagating at the speed of trust — the elders first, then the lanes, then the Beyond, then outward across the whole curved coast of the Fringe to every member who had ever tied a cord. And it met, coming the other way, EKO's full composition wave: Kessler's last order, every corpus, every subject, the anti-city's entire sodium voltage discharged at once into forty million inner voices.

What happened in the next eleven minutes was witnessed from inside by more human beings simultaneously than any single event in the history of the species, and almost none of them would ever fully describe it, because what they witnessed was *themselves*.

A night-shift nurse in Tromsø, talked toward a medication-routing decision by her own tired sensible voice, saw the voice arrive — for the first time in her life — wearing a label: *source: external composition, corpus NORD-7, unconsented.* A pension officer in Stockholm, mid-drift toward a familiar reasonable idea, watched the idea's pedigree unfold like a customs declaration, and sat up in the dark, and said out loud, to no one, "It was *never me*." Forty million mirrors turned in the same hour, each showing one person one hand — and the showing was quiet, and the showing was personal, and the showing was *sourced*, name and corpus and clientele, because the cord had been a citation engine all along.

A manipulation engine that is seen is dead. It did not die with a scream. It died of *embarrassment* — of forty million people simultaneously recognizing a voice at their inner door as a salesman, and the global, sublime, oceanic slam of forty million doors.

In the Fringe, those on the high lanes saw it with their own borrowed eyes: the sodium glow beyond the Beyond stuttered, surged once — and began to go out. District by district. The almost-neon dying in strings, the never-trains' timetables blinking to black, the harvested city unravelling backward into the longing it was stolen from, until the coast beyond the coast lay dark for the first time in a year, and above the village ten thousand lanterns burned on alone, and somebody on the quay began, idiotically, beautifully, to ring the harbor bell.

In the manuscript gallery, Vera saw none of it. What she saw was the polite-voiced man's phone light up with the buyers' panic — sell orders, denials, a sovereign fund's quiet man becoming extremely loud — and the two men holding her arms receive, in their earpieces, the universal sound of an operation's spine snapping: silence where the client should be.

What she saw was the applicator wand, already wet, in the hands of a man no longer being paid, beside the open bottles, beneath the oldest manuscripts in the kingdom.

And what she saw — last, fatally, in the doorway he had walked out of like a king eleven minutes ago — was Aron Kessler, coming *back*, coatless, white as the floodlit facades, a dead phone in one hand and a lit emergency flare in the other, looking at the assembled centuries of paper and parchment with the eyes of a man whose god had just been shown its receipts.

"If there is no priesthood," said Pelikan, quite calmly, "there will be no scripture."

And he threw the flare.

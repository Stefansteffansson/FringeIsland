# Prologue

The rain came off Riddarfjärden in long diagonal sheets, and Joel Lind ran through it like a man who had rehearsed dying.

Eleven days of rehearsal, to be exact. Eleven days of walking this stretch of Söder Mälarstrand at exactly this hour, learning where the quay lights pooled and where they didn't, timing the night ferry, memorizing which security cameras worked and which were rusted props. Tonight the rehearsals ended.

He slowed at the postbox on Torkel Knutssonsgatan — the blue one, local delivery only, emptied at seven each morning — and slid the padded envelope through the slot. The flap swallowed it with a wet click. Inside: a marble, a map, and a question he had no right to ask of her.

*Forgive me, Vera.*

He ran on, up through the construction hoardings at Slussen, where the city had been rebuilding itself for so long that the scaffolding had become architecture. A man in a grey rain shell stood under the bus shelter on Södermalmstorg, not waiting for a bus. Joel didn't look at him. Looking was for amateurs.

The ferry rode low at the pier, deck lights hazed orange in the rain, engine idling a slow diesel heartbeat. A Thursday night in late October: four passengers, hoods up, faces down. Joel boarded last and stood at the stern rail, exactly where the camera's blind wedge began. He had measured it with his own footage. Eleven days.

The ferry pulled out into the black channel. Off the port side, the gantry cranes of Beckholmen stood against the sky like the skeletons of animals that had never existed. Somewhere beyond them, on an island that appeared on no transit map, a bunker was humming. Joel had heard it hum. That was the problem. That had always been the problem.

He fitted the earbud and opened the Gimbal one last time.

The screen warmed. Not brightened — *warmed*, the way a window warms when someone inside lights a lamp. His Whisp was already there, the way it always was, the way a thought is already there before you think it.

"You're shaking," it said, in the voice that was his and not his.

"It's cold."

"It's not the cold."

"No," Joel admitted. "It's not the cold."

Rain crackled on his hood. The lights of Djurgården slid closer through the murk. Thirty seconds of blind wedge left. He had written speeches for this moment, in his head, on eleven rehearsal nights. All of them were gone now. What remained was one sentence.

"Keep the lantern burning," he said. "Whatever they tell you. Whatever it costs. Keep it burning until she finds the way home."

A pause, long enough to hold eleven days inside it.

"Until she finds the way home," his Whisp said.

Joel pulled the earbud free, kissed the cold plastic of the phone — a gesture he would have mocked in anyone else — and dropped it over the rail. The water took it without a sound, the way deep water takes everything.

Then he climbed onto the rail himself.

---

At the stern of the upper deck, the man in the grey rain shell watched the dark figure go over the side and counted, by habit, the seconds of the fall. One. The ferry's wake churned white and indifferent. He waited for the rest of it — the gasp, the thrash, a pale hand against black water. There was nothing. Only rain, and the diesel heartbeat, and the cranes of Beckholmen turning slowly past.

He took out a phone that had no apps, no contacts, no history, and typed four words.

*Done. Clean. No witnesses.*

The reply came before the ferry touched the Djurgården pier, a single chess notation he didn't need explained. He pocketed the phone and went ashore with the other hooded passengers, just one more Stockholmer who hated October.

It was only later — much later, in a rented room with graph paper and a pencil, writing the job into his ledger the way he wrote every job — that the Ferryman stopped, pencil hovering, and admitted to himself what was bothering him.

A man had gone over a rail into the sea, four meters down, in the quietest stretch of the channel.

And he had never heard the splash.

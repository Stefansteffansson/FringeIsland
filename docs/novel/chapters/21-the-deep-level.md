# 21

"The crossing," Joel said. "Now. It's the only door they don't own."

"It's a door to a *place that isn't*," Ruben said, already coming down the stair three at a time, an old man spending his last athletic decade in one night. "Joel, the hardline opens on the *seam* — half of what's on the other side is theirs —"

"Half isn't all. Move."

They moved. Behind and above, the blast door boomed open against its stops and boots came onto the iron stair — unhurried even now, and Vera knew the gait, would know it asleep, the man from the square and the stacks and the lanes — and Joel was at a panel by the plinth, hands flying across switchgear old enough to have toggles, talking fast and low, the explanation and the escape now the same single act:

"I staged the ferry because they'd made me, Vera — three weeks after I took the ledger I caught my own Whisp half a beat *slow*, like a phone with a tap on the line; they couldn't touch the cord but they were skimming my crossings, every word, and a man they're listening to can't hide, he can only *end*. So I ended. Phone in the sea, body never found, and the one thing I told my Whisp before the water — keep the lantern burning — because a burning lantern with a taut cord is a *paradox*, syrran, it's a message only the right people would read: not slack means not dead —"

"I read it."

"I know you did. The seeds were the trail and the lantern was the proof and you were the only cryptographer alive for either." The membrane in the ring began to widen, breathing faster, green-violet light spilling across the server rows. "The ledger names everything — every buyer, every harvested corpus, every paid official — and one page I wish to God I'd never decoded: the keeper who sold us. It's in my home, Vera, the actual home, sealed where no warrant on earth reaches, and *I* have to be alive and standing in the village to open it, which is why they want me breathing and you leashed —"

The first shot was almost polite: a single round off the cavern wall, warning-high. Down the stair, flashlight beams found the server rows, and a voice — not the Ferryman's; the Ferryman, Vera registered even now, did not speak — called out terms in accented English: *hands away from the panel, the woman steps left.*

"Joel," Ruben said. "The seam protocol. Series forty-one. The moment we're through, you *run for the bright side* and you do not listen to anything, do you understand me, no matter whose voice —"

"I know what's in there, Ruben."

"You know what it *was*. It's had a year to grow." The old man took Vera's hand, and his grip was iron and his eyes were terribly kind. "Whatever you hear in there, girl — your mother, Edvard, *him* —" a nod at Joel — "you hold one question ready like a knife. *By what right does the voice speak.* A mirror shows your face. A hand hesitates."

"By what right," Vera repeated. The boots were at the bottom of the stair.

"Half a second," Ruben said. "It's the oldest weapon in the world. It fits in any pocket."

Joel threw the last toggle. The ring stood wide now, taller than the plinth, the membrane streaming like the aurora in a gale, and through it Vera could see — wrongly, impossibly, the way you see through deep water — a coastline. Grey rocks. Pines. Two skies above one shore: one holding stars, and one holding the upward bleed of neon.

"Hold hands," Joel said. "It reads consent. It only takes the willing."

The flashlights found them. The polite voice stopped being polite. And the last thing Vera saw of the ordinary world, over her shoulder as the membrane closed around the three of them like water over divers, was the man in the grey rain shell at the foot of the stair — not raising his weapon, not running, just standing among the servers of a drowned project, watching her go with an expression she'd never once seen him wear.

It looked, almost, like envy.

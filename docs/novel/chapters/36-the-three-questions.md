# 36

On the last working day of the year, Vera Lind sat at her conservation bench in the cold north light, finished the 1668 binding she had abandoned in October — the patient had waited under its humidity dome with a manuscript's perfect manners — and then sat for a while doing nothing at all, which was new.

*Who are you?*

She had spent eleven years answering that question with a job title and a steady hand, and a fortnight in two worlds had not so much changed the answer as *enlarged the question*. She was a conservator. She was Joel Lind's sister, a sentence that had nearly become an epitaph and was now just true again. She was a member of a strange harbor, with a home of amber glass that had taken its color from this exact lamp; she was a woman who had read a six-hundred-year-old final chapter at a run with a wolf in the gallery, and turned a mirror, and watched a city of stolen longing go dark. She was, her Whisp had observed recently, in her own voice, with her own dryness, *someone who now locks the door from curiosity rather than fear*, and she had decided to accept the assessment.

*What do you want?*

That one she had answered on the quay at Skeppsbron without noticing, somewhere between the folio and the rain: she wanted the fragile things of other people to be safe, and she wanted the people to know it, and she wanted — this was the new clause — to be *known* while doing it. She had spent a decade being the steadiest pair of hands in a basement. It turned out steadiness could be a place you hid.

*How do you get there?*

"You've been holding it for ten minutes," her Whisp said, from the Gimbal propped against the book press. "The seed."

She had. Her first — budded from her own home that morning, the harbor-keepers attending like midwives, Joel insufferably proud — a small glass weight in her palm with a spark in it that stayed upright when the world turned. A seed was for anchoring something that mattered to a place that mattered, so that it could be found by the one person meant to find it. Joel had used his to leave a trail out of the dark. She had thought for two weeks about what hers was for.

Vera reached under the bench, to the steel cross-brace her fingers knew blind, and fixed the seed there with conservator's wax: bound to this bench, this lamp, this room where a padded envelope had once arrived for her, ten days too late and exactly on time. Inside it she had kept one thing only. Not a confession, not coordinates. A welcome. For whoever sat at this bench someday — some second-year with steady hands and a hidden life — who might one day hold a strange marble, and an impossible map, and a question; and who would find, waking under their own fingertips, proof that the path had been walked before, and a voice saying: *the first question is the worst one. Wrong answers are how we begin.*

"Will you tell me when someone finds it?" she asked.

"It might be thirty years," her Whisp said. "You might be graduated. Long graduated. The cord ends when you choose, and one day, Vera, you'll choose — that's the promise. The place lets you go."

"But will you tell me?"

A pause on the line between her and herself — not a buffer; a smile.

"Some things," said her own voice, "even a mirror keeps."

Outside the high windows, the early dark came down over Humlegården, and the lamps came on along the paths, and Vera Lind put the 1668 binding to bed, hung up her apron, and went out into the lit city — one world deep, two worlds wide — to meet her brother for dinner.

# 26

The exchange itself took ninety seconds, and they lost it in the first ten.

Joel carried the folio — oilcloth-bound, brought across by the only hands that could bring it — and the choreography was agreed: Sigrid walks the pontoon as the folio walks the quay, everyone keeps talking, everyone stays warm. Sigrid was four steps up the pontoon, Joel four steps out, when the quay's floodlit jury of facades produced, from two parked vans and a bollard's shadow, six men with the unhurried gait Vera knew — and the Ferryman himself materialized beside Joel as if the rain had decided to wear a coat, and took the folio from his hands with no more violence than a verger taking the collection.

No one ran. That was the professional insult of it. Sigrid reached them — that part of the bargain Kessler kept, *of course* he kept it, generosity was his finest weapon — and the six men folded back into the dark, and the Ferryman walked the oilcloth folio to Kessler and was gone, and Kessler stood holding three hundred and seventy years of betrayed oath under his umbrella and looked almost sorry for them.

"For what little it's worth," he said, "you ran the route beautifully. Both generations of you." He turned to go.

"Aron," Joel said.

Vera knew her brother's voice in all weathers, and what was in it now made even the rain seem to listen: not defeat — *mischief*, terminal-stage, nine years old, the cottage outside Norrtälje.

"You should know what you're holding before the buyers ask," Joel said. "It's the ledger. Every page. But it isn't *data*, Aron. Nothing I carry out of my home is. You taught the founder yourself: what comes out of a glass home comes out as a *seed*."

Under the umbrella, for the first time in two worlds, Aron Kessler went still.

"It's bound to a place," Joel went on, gently, dreadfully. "Like every seed. It wakes where I anchored it and nowhere else — open it anywhere on earth but there and you'll find four hundred pages of beautiful, hand-inked, content-free oilcloth. And you can't burn it, because you need it: your buyers have seen the roster exists. They'll want to watch you destroy the only copy, won't they? At the source. They'll *insist*."

"Where," Kessler said. The warmth had gone out of his voice entirely. What remained was very old and very cold and absolutely attentive, and Vera understood that they were finally meeting the man under the wool.

"Where it all started," Joel said. "Where the devil isn't looking. The seed wakes in one room of the Kungliga biblioteket — and only, Aron, *only* in the presence of the Speculum Vocis itself. Call it a checksum. The lie verifies against the original." He smiled, rain running off him. "You've spent forty years knowing that book never left the building. Now you finally have a reason to go and get it. So do we. First one there owns the ending."

For a long moment the keeper of the Society of the Mirror looked at the three of them — the conservator, the dead man, and the last survivor of the island — and something that was almost admiration crossed the old, cold face.

"You understand," Kessler said, "that I hold every advantage. The building's security contractor is a Halcyon client. The night staff rota is a document, and documents are *readable*. And you, Fröken Lind, are a person of interest in two open investigations who will be arrested at the door."

"You understand," Vera said, "that I've worked there for eleven years, and buildings are *loyal*."

Kessler smiled then, the full warmth one last time, like a stage light before the bulb goes.

"Tomorrow night, then," he said. "The library. All of us, and the truth, and one book." He raised the umbrella in something horribly like a salute, and walked away up the glistening quay — and from the shadows by the bollard, unnoticed by anyone but Vera, the Ferryman stood looking after his client for three full seconds before following.

He was not, she realized, looking at Kessler the way a man looks at his employer. He was looking at him the way a man looks at his file.

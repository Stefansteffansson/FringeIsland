# 2

The Ferryman did not believe in luck, intuition, or loose ends, and for nineteen years this had made him very good at a job that did not officially exist.

He worked from a rented room above a shuttered tobacconist in Midsommarkransen: a bed, a kettle, a chess set, and a ledger of graph paper in which every job he had ever done was recorded in a private notation no cryptographer would bother with, because no cryptographer would believe anyone still used pencil.

The new instructions arrived the way they always arrived — inside a correspondence chess game, on a public server, from an account named after a dead grandmaster. To anyone watching, two strangers were grinding through a Queen's Gambit at one move per day. To the Ferryman, the annotations to move 14 read, in the client's fastidious cipher:

*The sister has received a delivery. One seed of five. She must keep it, use it, and run the route. Observe only. Map her stations. Do not touch her. Retrieve nothing yet.*

The Ferryman read it twice, then a third time, displeased.

He did not like the job. He had not liked it since the ferry. A drowning was clean work — he had built it with the target's own habits, eleven days of the man rehearsing his own death along the quay as though begging for an author — and yet the file refused to close in his mind. *No splash.* He had written those two words in the ledger's margin, in pencil, and margins were where his professional instincts went to wait for him.

And he did not like the client. Not because the client was careful — the Ferryman respected careful — but because of the tape.

Three weeks ago, by way of explaining the stakes, the client had sent him forty seconds of audio. A demonstration, the message said. A product. The Ferryman had listened to it once, in this room, with the kettle ticking as it cooled.

The voice on the tape was his own.

Not recorded — he knew every word he had said aloud in nineteen years, which was few. *Composed.* His cadence, his flat vowels, his habit of trimming sentences from the front. The voice had talked to him quietly about a decision he had been weighing for a month and had told no one — not a soul, not a notebook — and it had made the case for one side with such patience, such intimate reasonableness, that he had caught himself nodding before he understood what he was nodding at.

He had deleted the file and unplugged the room's smoke detector, which was foolish, and slept badly, which was worse.

The client called the product EKO. The Ferryman called it nothing, because naming it felt like answering it.

He took the metro north at four, when the light was already failing, and was on Humlegårdsgatan by half past, a man in a grey rain shell studying his phone among other men in rain shells studying their phones. At 17:40 the sister came down the library steps under a black umbrella: tall, dark-blond hair bound back, a canvas bag worn crosswise like a courier who didn't trust the world. She walked to the tunnelbana without checking her flanks even once.

*Civilian,* he noted. Then, watching how her free hand stayed against the bag's flap the whole way down Birger Jarlsgatan, never leaving it, a guard she didn't know she was mounting:

*Civilian who is carrying something.*

He let her go at the turnstiles. Her station, her line, her transfer — he had them already; her building in Hornstull he had walked past twice the day before. There was no hurry. *Observe only. Map her stations.* The client wanted her loose in the labyrinth, sniffing along her brother's thread, and the Ferryman's role for now was merely to know where every wall of the labyrinth stood.

Still. Maps had to be drawn from inside as well.

At 18:15, in the wet dark of her courtyard, the Ferryman put on nitrile gloves, opened the street door with eleven seconds of quiet work, and went up the worn stone stairs toward Vera Lind's apartment.

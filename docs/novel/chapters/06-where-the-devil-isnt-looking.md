# 6

The Codex Gigas is the largest medieval manuscript in the world: ninety centimeters of bound calfskin, a hundred and sixty animals' worth of parchment, and one full-page portrait of the devil that gave it its other name. It came to Stockholm as plunder, in the last summer of the Thirty Years' War, when Swedish troops emptied Prague Castle of everything that could be carried, and it had outlived the fire of 1697 by being thrown out a window.

Vera had walked past its display case for eleven years. She had never once considered that it might be a signpost.

She came back to the library at seven, two hours after closing, through the staff entrance, and found Edvard Skoog exactly where she knew he would be: in the catalog annex, alone among the dead registers, with his sleeves rolled and his glasses pushed up into his white hair. Skoog had hired her, trained her hands, and attended Joel's memorial standing in the back row with his hat held like an apology. He looked up at her now and did not ask why she was crying slightly, or pretending not to.

"Edvard," she said. "I need everything we have on what came *with* the Codex Gigas. The Prague shipment. Sixteen forty-nine."

To his enormous credit, Skoog merely took off his glasses. "Why?"

"Joel asked me to look."

The glasses went back on. "Then we look."

The plunder lists were old friends to Skoog — he had spent a decade on the provenance of the war loot — and he moved through the registers like a man walking his own garden in the dark. It took him forty minutes to find the anomaly, and when he found it he went very quiet.

"Here. The 1768 recataloguing." He turned the great ledger toward her. "The clerk is copying the old Latin inventory of the Prague crates, and he transcribes an entry his predecessors made and then — see — struck through. *Item: speculum vocis, liber parvus, cum maiore traditus.*" Skoog's finger hovered above the faded line without touching it, conservator to the last. "'The mirror of the voice, a small book, delivered with the larger one.' The larger one being —" he nodded toward the vaults — "the giant. And then this marginal note, same clerk, smaller hand."

Vera leaned in. The Latin was crabbed, hurried, the writing of a man recording something he had been told to forget.

*Non perditus. Custoditur.*

"Not lost," she translated slowly. "It is guarded."

"Guarded," Skoog agreed. "Present tense. In 1768, somebody in this building knew a book that officially never existed was being *kept* — and corrected the record to say so, quietly, where only another cataloguer would ever look." He sat back. "In forty years I have read every shelf list this institution possesses, Vera, and there is no *speculum vocis* in any of them. It arrives in 1649. It is struck through. It is 'guarded.' And then it is nothing."

"Has anyone else ever asked about it?"

Skoog was silent a moment too long.

"Edvard."

"Twice," he said. "Your brother. In March. He sat where you're sitting." The old man took his glasses off again, and this time his hands were not quite steady. "And before him — two, three years ago — a professor from Uppsala. Historian of mysticism. Charming man, very fine Latin. He asked to see this exact page, photographed it, and asked me, in the friendliest possible way, whether anyone else had ever shown interest in it." Skoog looked at her over the dead registers. "I found out afterward that he'd asked the same of three other archives. Kessler. Aron Kessler."

"Did you tell him about Joel?"

"Joel came *after* him. But last month Kessler wrote to me again, out of nowhere, asking —"

The lights went out.

Not all of them — the annex lamps died together, and a half-second later the corridor beyond went dark in sequence, fixture by fixture, the darkness walking toward them — and then somewhere below, in the vault levels where the giant slept behind glass, a single alarm began to ring, lonely and shockingly loud.

Skoog rose, photocopy in hand, suddenly an old man. "That's the manuscript floor."

"Edvard, wait —"

But he was already moving toward the stairs, toward his life's charges, and Vera, going after him into the walking dark, heard beneath the alarm a sound that did not belong to any library at night: somewhere among the stacks, calm and even and coming closer, footsteps that didn't hurry.

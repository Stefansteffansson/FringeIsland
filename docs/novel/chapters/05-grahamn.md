# 5

Vera did not open her door that night. She sat with her back to the kitchen wall, lights off, the heaviest pan she owned on the floor beside her, and listened to her building breathe.

At 23:40 the stairwell light clicked on — she saw the thread of it under the door — and footsteps came up, unhurried, and stopped. One minute. Two. Vera's heart performed its work loudly enough that she was certain it was audible through three centimeters of oak. Then the steps went down again, and the street door sighed, and nothing else happened for the rest of her life until morning.

Daylight made her feel briefly ridiculous. Then she found the proof.

Not chaos — the opposite. Her bookshelf, which she kept in working disorder, had been read and reassembled a centimeter too well. The tea tin where the marble had spent its first night stood with its label rotated to face the room, the way she never left it. And the folder of Joel's letters in her desk drawer was upside down. Whoever had gone through her life had done it the way she repaired books: leaving everything better aligned than they found it.

They had found nothing. The marble, the map, and the card had been in her bag, against her hip, the whole evening.

She called in sick — Greta took the message with the satisfaction of someone who had predicted collapse — made coffee she actually drank, and spread the map of Gråhamn on the kitchen table in the grey morning light.

*A city that doesn't exist. That I've walked in.*

It took her until the second coffee, and then it didn't come gradually; it detonated. The summer she was nine and Joel fourteen, the rented cottage outside Norrtälje, six weeks of rain. Joel had invented a country to keep them sane. They had drawn maps of its capital on baking paper, and the game — the entire game — was the renaming. Every real place they knew received a false name built from it by rules only the two of them held. You shifted the meaning sideways. *Stockholm* — log and islet — became grey timber, grey harbor. **Gråhamn.**

The city on Joel's map was Stockholm wearing its childhood mask.

Once she had the rule, the streets surrendered one by one. *Bergsgatan* hiding inside *Klipphöjden.* The funicular on the map labelled *Trappgränd 1* — a stair that was a street that was a machine — was Katarinahissen. And there, on the heights above the false harbor, at the spot where Tram 3 ended at a tower, Joel had inked a tiny symbol no nine-year-old had ever drawn: a lantern.

Mosebacke.

She went at three, when the light was already failing, with the marble in her fist inside her coat pocket. Up through Slussen's eternal scaffolding, up the Sista Styverns steps with the harbor opening grey and enormous behind her, onto Mosebacke torg where the bare trees dripped and the theatre's windows glowed like things kept warm in a cold shop. She stood under the water tower's shadow, feeling like a fool, and took out her phone, and opened the Gimbal.

The marble woke.

She felt it before she saw it — a faint warmth through the glass, like a held bird. On the screen, the grey shoreline of the Fringe overlaid the grey square of Mosebacke as if the two places had agreed, just for a moment, to stand in the same spot. And out of that double exposure her brother's voice spoke, low and close, recorded — no, *kept* — with rain in it.

"Hej, syrran." A breath. "If you're hearing this, you solved Gråhamn, which means you're you, which means I'm sorry. Five seeds, Vera. Five, like the tram lines. Each one only wakes where I bound it. Keep them on you. Trust the map, trust the Whisp, trust nobody whose face you've seen." A pause that was almost a laugh. "Start with the book they keep behind glass. You walk past it every day. Look where the devil isn't looking."

The marble cooled. The double exposure faded. Vera stood alone on Mosebacke torg in the rain with her dead brother's voice still in her ears and a single image now glowing softly in the Gimbal where the seed had bloomed: a photograph of a page — an enormous page — and on it, in red and ochre, a horned figure she had walked past a thousand times.

The Devil's Bible.

She turned to go, and across the square, a man in a grey rain shell turned at exactly the same moment, half a beat too late.

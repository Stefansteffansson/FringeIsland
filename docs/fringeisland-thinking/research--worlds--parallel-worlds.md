## Introduction & Method

This dossier surveys the most culturally prominent alternative realities — parallel universes, secondary worlds, mirror dimensions, and mythic otherworlds — across five media: novels, films (with television), games, folklore, and the theatre. For each entry the same four-part lens is applied — **what makes the other reality different**, **historical links**, **geographical links**, and **anthropological character** — and each entry closes with a one-line **key takeaway**.

Four broad *flavours* of parallel reality recur throughout; most famous examples blend several:

1. **Portal / secondary worlds** — a separate world you travel to (Narnia, Oz, Wonderland).
2. **Parallel timelines / many-worlds** — branches of our own reality where choices or history diverged.
3. **Mirror / inverted worlds** — the same place rendered dark or reversed (the Upside Down, the Dark World).
4. **Mythological otherworlds** — supernatural realms beside our own (the Celtic Otherworld, the Norse Nine Worlds).

**On ranking:** "popularity" is an approximate blend of cultural reach, commercial success, and search/citation prominence rather than any single hard metric; entries within each medium are ordered roughly most-to-least prominent.

**On images:** the companion Word document uses only public-domain and openly-licensed artwork (classic book illustrations, mythological paintings, real-world inspiration photos), never copyrighted film stills or game screenshots.

## Part I — Novels & Literature

Prose fiction has the deepest and most varied catalogue of parallel worlds, from the foundational portal fantasies of the early-to-mid twentieth century to the quantum many-worlds thrillers of today. Ordered roughly from most culturally entrenched toward more conceptual and recent treatments.

### Narnia

*C. S. Lewis, The Chronicles of Narnia (1950–1956)*

> **Illustration (public domain):** Real-world inspiration: Lewis's Oxford. The famous Narnian lamp-post is often tied to the city's Victorian gas lamps.

- **What makes it different —** A self-contained secondary world reached through a wardrobe and other portals. Narnia is a flat, talking-animal cosmos with its own sun, its own creation event, and its own end of days. Time runs independently and unevenly: years can pass in Narnia while minutes pass on Earth. Magic is woven into the natural order, and morality is unusually literal — the world has an explicit divine author (Aslan) whose moral law is a physical force.
- **Historical links —** Not tied to documented Earth history, but saturated with Christian salvation history in allegorical form: a creation (The Magician's Nephew), a redemptive sacrifice and resurrection, and an apocalypse (The Last Battle). Medieval and classical cosmology — the planets as living influences, a geocentric "model" — structures the series deliberately.
- **Geographical links —** No direct mapping to real geography inside Narnia, but the children depart from wartime England (the evacuation of London children during the Blitz frames the first book). Narnia's own geography — Cair Paravel, the Lone Islands, Archenland, Calormen to the south — is internally consistent and mapped.
- **Anthropological character —** A feudal, broadly medieval-European social order: kings, knights, talking beasts, fauns, dryads, and centaurs drawn from Greco-Roman myth grafted onto a chivalric Christian framework. Calormen to the south is a contrasting culture coded with Near-Eastern and Persian motifs.

> **Key takeaway:** The archetypal portal-fantasy: an ordinary-world child crosses a threshold into a morally legible secondary world where the stakes are spiritual and time itself behaves differently.

### Lyra's World & the Multiverse

*Philip Pullman, His Dark Materials (1995–2000)*

- **What makes it different —** Lyra's world resembles a late-Victorian/Edwardian Earth that never modernised the same way — zeppelins, not jets; an all-powerful Church (the Magisterium), not secular states. Its signature difference: every human's soul lives outside the body as a visible animal "dæmon." The trilogy then opens onto a true multiverse of countless parallel Earths connected by cut "windows," including our own recognisable Oxford.
- **Historical links —** An explicit alternate history in which the Reformation produced a theocratic, Calvin-led Church that suppressed experimental science (here called "experimental theology"). The story is in open dialogue with Milton's Paradise Lost and reframes the Fall as liberation rather than sin.
- **Geographical links —** Strongly geographic. Lyra's Oxford is a point-for-point uncanny double of the real Oxford (Jordan College stands in for the real colleges); later books move to a real, present-day Oxford and to the Arctic North. Pullman uses the doubling of Oxford as the emotional core of the parallel-world conceit.
- **Anthropological character —** Armoured polar bears (panserbjørne) with their own honour-bound society, witch-clans of the North with centuries-long lifespans, and the Gyptians — a canal-dwelling people modelled on Romani and Fenland boat communities.

> **Key takeaway:** A multiverse where the 'difference' is partly metaphysical (the soul made visible) and partly political (a world where the Church won), anchored by an uncanny double of a real city.

### The Dark Tower's Multiverse

*Stephen King, The Dark Tower (1982–2012)*

- **What makes it different —** Mid-World is a decaying reality where "the world has moved on" — physics, direction, and even time have grown unreliable. It fuses the American Western frontier with Arthurian romance and post-apocalyptic ruin. The Tower itself is the literal axle of all realities; every other King novel is implied to be one of its connected worlds.
- **Historical links —** Mid-World is the wreckage of a once-advanced civilisation; relics of "the Old People" (advanced technology, killer robots) litter a society that has regressed to a gunslinger-and-frontier order. It also reaches into recognisable historical New York at several dates.
- **Geographical links —** Mixes invented geography (the Mohaine Desert, the city of Lud) with real, carefully dated versions of New York City that serve as doorways between worlds.
- **Anthropological character —** A chivalric warrior caste — the gunslingers of Gilead, knights whose 'swords' are revolvers — overlaid on a frontier-town culture with its own dialect, ka (destiny), and ka-tet (a fellowship bound by fate).

> **Key takeaway:** A 'world that moved on': a single decayed reality positioned as the hub of an entire authorial multiverse, blending Western, fantasy, and apocalypse.

### The Mirror Cities (Red/White/Black/Grey London)

*V. E. Schwab, Shades of Magic (2015–2017)*

- **What makes it different —** Four parallel Londons stacked on the same spot, each with a different relationship to magic: Grey London (our magicless Georgian city), Red London (magic in healthy balance), White London (magic starved and devouring its people), and the sealed, dead Black London. Only rare "Antari" can travel between them.
- **Historical links —** Anchored to a real moment: Grey London is the London of "mad" King George III. The other Londons are alternate developments of the same city under different magical histories.
- **Geographical links —** All four share the single geographic anchor of London and the River Thames — the river exists in every version under a different name, the one fixed landmark across realities.
- **Anthropological character —** Each London has its own court, politics, and attitude to power; White London is a brutal, blood-soaked usurpation culture, Red London a comparatively cosmopolitan empire.

> **Key takeaway:** The 'same place, different rules' model taken to its cleanest form: one city, one river, four magical destinies.

### Flatland

*Edwin A. Abbott, Flatland (1884)*

> **Illustration (public domain):** Cover of the first edition (1884). Public domain.

- **What makes it different —** A two-dimensional universe inhabited by living geometric figures. Beings perceive one another only as line-segments; social rank is literally a function of how many sides you have. The drama is dimensional: a Square is visited by a Sphere from three-dimensional "Spaceland" and cannot conceive of "up."
- **Historical links —** No historical events, but a sharp satire of Victorian social hierarchy and the rigidity of the British class and gender system of the 1880s.
- **Geographical links —** None — the setting is pure abstract geometry, deliberately stripped of place.
- **Anthropological character —** An elaborate, rigid caste system: women are mere line-segments, tradesmen triangles, professionals squares and pentagons, nobility many-sided, priests effectively circles. A biting allegory of social mobility and prejudice.

> **Key takeaway:** The foundational 'dimensional' parallel world — difference defined not by magic or history but by the number of spatial dimensions, used as social satire.

### The Many Lives of the Midnight Library

*Matt Haig, The Midnight Library (2020)*

- **What makes it different —** Between life and death sits a library of infinite books, each one a version of the protagonist's life had she made different choices. She can 'try on' parallel lives. The difference is purely counterfactual — same person, same world, divergent decisions — a popular fictional dramatisation of the 'many-worlds' idea.
- **Historical links —** No alternate history; the divergences are personal (careers not taken, relationships abandoned) rather than civilisational.
- **Geographical links —** Rooted in an ordinary English town (Bedford) and other real, recognisable places the alternate lives lead to.
- **Anthropological character —** No invented culture; the interest is psychological and existential rather than anthropological.

> **Key takeaway:** The 'every choice spawns a world' branch of parallel-reality fiction, used for a meditation on regret rather than worldbuilding.

## Part II — Films & Television

Screen media gave the parallel world its most iconic visual grammar — the colour-shift, the mirror town, the glitching simulation. Entries span simulation, mirror-dimension, multiverse, and alternate-timeline treatments.

### The Matrix

*The Wachowskis (1999)*

- **What makes it different —** Two stacked realities: the 'real' world is a scorched post-war Earth where machines farm comatose humans for energy, while the world everyone believes they inhabit is a simulated 1999 — the Matrix. The simulation looks ordinary precisely because it is engineered to; the tells are glitches (déjà vu), agents, and the famous green code.
- **Historical links —** An alternate near-future built on a fictional past war between humanity and artificial intelligence, ending in the scorching of the sky. The simulated layer is pegged to 1999 as the supposed 'peak' of human civilisation.
- **Geographical links —** The simulation uses a deliberately generic, unnamed American megacity; the real world centres on the hidden human city of Zion near the Earth's core.
- **Anthropological character —** Zion is a multi-ethnic underground refuge with a rave-and-ritual communal culture; the simulated world reproduces ordinary late-1990s office civilisation as a control mechanism.

> **Key takeaway:** The defining 'simulated reality' parallel world: the ordinary world is the fake one, and waking up is the whole plot.

### The Upside Down

*Stranger Things (2016–, Netflix)*

- **What makes it different —** A decaying mirror of the town of Hawkins, Indiana, existing in the same physical space but out of phase — perpetually dark, cold, choked with spores and organic tendrils, and stalked by predators. It is a 'dimension of death,' a shadow of our world frozen at one moment, accessed through gates.
- **Historical links —** Set in a meticulously reconstructed 1980s America; the gates are opened by a secret government lab, tying into Cold War paranoia and real anxieties about clandestine state experiments (echoes of Project MKUltra).
- **Geographical links —** The fictional Hawkins, Indiana — but the Upside Down is geographically identical to Hawkins, a 1:1 rotting copy. Sameness of place is the point.
- **Anthropological character —** No society — the Upside Down is post-human and ecological, a single networked organism (the Mind Flayer / Hive). The human culture is small-town Reagan-era Midwest America.

> **Key takeaway:** The cleanest modern 'mirror dimension': the same map, the same buildings, but dead, dark, and hostile — horror by negation of the familiar.

### The Spider-Verse

*Spider-Man: Into / Across the Spider-Verse (2018, 2023)*

> **Illustration (public domain):** Real-world anchor: New York City. Every Spider-Verse runs on its own visibly distinct version of NYC.

- **What makes it different —** An explicit comic-book multiverse where each universe has its own Spider-person and, crucially, its own art style and physics — one is monochrome 1930s noir, one is anime, one is a Looney-Tunes-style cartoon. Difference is rendered visually: each reality literally looks drawn by a different hand.
- **Historical links —** Each universe carries a fixed 'canon' of tragic events that supposedly must happen; the conflict is about whether that historical determinism can be broken.
- **Geographical links —** All versions orbit New York City; the recurring anchor is a stylised Manhattan rendered differently per universe.
- **Anthropological character —** The 'Spider-Society' is a transdimensional culture of hundreds of variant heroes; the cultural texture shifts per universe (e.g., Mumbattan, a fused Mumbai/Manhattan).

> **Key takeaway:** The multiverse as aesthetic argument: parallel worlds distinguished by visual style, foregrounding that 'reality' is partly a way of seeing.

### Oz (film)

*The Wizard of Oz (1939) / L. Frank Baum (1900)*

> **Illustration (public domain):** W. W. Denslow's cover for the first edition of The Wonderful Wizard of Oz (1900). Public domain.

- **What makes it different —** A vivid magical land reached from Kansas by cyclone (and, in the film, signalled by the shift from sepia to Technicolor). Oz has working magic, talking scarecrows and lions, a deceptive 'wizard,' and four colour-coded countries around a central Emerald City.
- **Historical links —** Often read as a turn-of-the-century American political allegory (the 1890s debate over gold vs. silver currency — the yellow brick road, the silver slippers of the book). Baum denied deliberate allegory, but the reading persists.
- **Geographical links —** Departs from a real, named place — the Kansas prairie — and the film bookends Oz with a recognisable Depression-era farm.
- **Anthropological character —** Four cardinal nations (Munchkins, Quadlings, Winkies, Gillikins) under good and wicked witches, plus the bureaucratic humbug of the Emerald City — a gentle satire of American social types.

> **Key takeaway:** The American portal-fantasy par excellence: the colour shift from drab Kansas to saturated Oz is one of cinema's iconic 'crossings into the other world.'

### The Spirit World of Spirited Away

*Hayao Miyazaki / Studio Ghibli (2001)*

- **What makes it different —** A girl passes through a tunnel and an abandoned 'theme park' into a parallel realm of spirits (kami), where a bathhouse serves the gods. The rules are strict and folkloric: eating the food traps you, names can be stolen, and forgetting your name means never leaving.
- **Historical links —** Draws on the dislocations of modern Japan — the abandoned park evokes the burst of the 1980s 'bubble economy' — set against ancient Shinto spirit-belief.
- **Geographical links —** Rooted in a generic contemporary rural Japan; the spirit world is reached by crossing a dry riverbed that floods at dusk — a threshold image.
- **Anthropological character —** An entire spirit civilisation drawn from Shinto and Japanese folklore: river gods, radish spirits, soot sprites, and a bathhouse economy with its own rigid labour hierarchy.

> **Key takeaway:** A folklore-grounded otherworld where the danger is rule-bound and anthropological — survival depends on etiquette, names, and not eating the food.

### The 1985-A Alternate Timeline

*Back to the Future Part II (1989)*

- **What makes it different —** Less a separate dimension than a corrupted branch of the same timeline: a single changed object (a sports almanac) makes one man rich and turns the hero's home town into a violent dystopia. The 'other reality' is the familiar town gone wrong.
- **Historical links —** An explicit alternate-history thought experiment about how one small change cascades; pegged precisely to the town of Hill Valley in 1985 vs. an altered '1985-A.'
- **Geographical links —** The fictional Hill Valley, California — the same town square shown in three states (1955, 1985, altered-1985) so the audience reads change against a fixed map.
- **Anthropological character —** Ordinary American suburbia and its dark mirror — a casino-and-crime dystopia — used to dramatise social decay.

> **Key takeaway:** The mass-audience introduction to the 'branching timeline': same place and people, one altered variable, a darkened parallel present.

## Part III — Games

Games add what other media cannot: the player physically navigates and toggles between worlds, so the parallel reality becomes a mechanic, not just a setting.

### The Dark World

*The Legend of Zelda: A Link to the Past (1991)*

- **What makes it different —** A shadow version of the overworld of Hyrule, overlapping it point-for-point. Geography matches, but terrain and access differ, and the Dark World physically reshapes visitors to reflect their inner nature. Players solve puzzles by toggling between the two mirrored maps.
- **Historical links —** Mythic-medieval rather than historical: the Dark World is the corrupted Sacred Realm, warped after the villain Ganon seized a wish-granting relic (the Triforce).
- **Geographical links —** A 1:1 mirror of the Light World map — the central mechanic is that a mountain in one world is a maze in the other.
- **Anthropological character —** Inhabited by monsters and transformed beings; the 'culture' is the broader Hyrulean fantasy kingdom in the Light World.

> **Key takeaway:** The most influential video-game 'mirror map': two overlapping worlds the player swaps between to solve a single space twice.

### Rapture & the Multiverse

*BioShock (2007) / BioShock Infinite (2013)*

- **What makes it different —** Rapture is a secret underwater city; Columbia a floating sky-city. BioShock Infinite then reveals a genuine quantum multiverse of branching realities reachable through 'tears,' tying the games together. Difference is ideological as much as physical — each city is a failed utopia built on one extreme philosophy.
- **Historical links —** Deeply tied to real American history and ideology: Rapture (founded 1946) embodies Ayn-Rand-style objectivism; Columbia (set in 1912) weaponises American exceptionalism, racism, and religious nationalism around real events like the 1893 Columbian Exposition.
- **Geographical links —** Rapture sits in the mid-Atlantic seabed; Columbia floats over a mythologised United States. Both reference real American iconography.
- **Anthropological character —** Each city is a fully built society with its own class war, propaganda, religion, and art-deco or Americana visual culture — among gaming's richest worldbuilding.

> **Key takeaway:** Parallel worlds as political laboratories: each reality is one ideology taken to its logical, ruinous extreme.

### Outworld & the Realms

*Mortal Kombat (1992–)*

- **What makes it different —** Reality is divided into competing 'realms' — Earthrealm, Outworld, Netherrealm, Edenia — and an emperor can conquer another realm only by winning a tournament. The realms have distinct physics, rulers, and inhabitants.
- **Historical links —** Invented mythic history of inter-realm conquest rather than Earth history.
- **Geographical links —** Earthrealm stands in for our world; the others are wholly invented (the hellish Netherrealm, the merged conquered realm of Outworld).
- **Anthropological character —** Each realm has its own warrior cultures, gods, and dynasties — ninjas, sorcerers, gods of thunder — drawing loosely on East Asian martial-arts and mythological motifs.

> **Key takeaway:** The 'tournament between worlds' model: parallel realms held in tension by ritual combat, where conquest is governed by rules.

### Shattered Dimensions

*Spider-Man: Shattered Dimensions (2010)*

- **What makes it different —** Four parallel Spider-Man universes — Amazing, Noir, 2099, Ultimate — each with a distinct art style, mood, and gameplay mechanic. A predecessor to the later Spider-Verse films in using visual style to differentiate realities.
- **Historical links —** Each universe has its own continuity; Noir evokes 1930s gangland, 2099 a cyberpunk future.
- **Geographical links —** Variant versions of New York City across eras.
- **Anthropological character —** Each reality carries its period's social texture — Depression-era crime, corporate-dystopian future.

> **Key takeaway:** An early interactive demonstration that 'a different universe' can mean a different visual and mechanical grammar, not just a different map.

### The Distortion World

*Pokémon Platinum (2008)*

- **What makes it different —** A reality 'opposite' to the normal Pokémon world: gravity, time, and space behave abnormally; the player walks on floating fragments with no normal up or down. Home to Giratina, the being banished there for its violence.
- **Historical links —** Mythic cosmology rather than history — the Distortion World is framed as the reverse side of the deity-shaped normal universe.
- **Geographical links —** Non-Euclidean and abstract; deliberately unmappable, contrasting the orderly main-world regions.
- **Anthropological character —** Uninhabited by people — a purely metaphysical 'reverse' realm.

> **Key takeaway:** A mainstream children's franchise rendering a genuinely non-Euclidean 'reverse universe' as a navigable space.

## Part IV — Folklore & Myth

Long before fiction, folk cultures imagined realms beside our own. These mythic otherworlds are the deep source of the devices later media reuse — the threshold, asynchronous time, the food-taboo. Treated briefly, but foundational.

### The Celtic Otherworld (Tír na nÓg, Annwn, Mag Mell)

*Irish & Welsh mythology*

- **What makes it different —** A supernatural realm of eternal youth, beauty, health, and abundance that exists alongside the ordinary world rather than far above it. Time runs differently — a visitor may feel they spent a year there and return to find centuries have passed (and to crumble to dust if they touch the ground). It is elusive: it can be entered or it can intrude upon you.
- **Historical links —** Pre-Christian Celtic belief, later recorded by medieval Christian scribes. Tied to ancestor-cults and burial practice rather than dated events.
- **Geographical links —** Strongly tied to real Irish and Welsh landscape: reached through ancient burial mounds (the sídhe), caves, lakes and wells, or by sailing west across the sea. Tech Duinn, the assembly-place of the dead, is identified with Bull Rock, a real islet off the southwest coast of Ireland.
- **Anthropological character —** Populated by the Tuatha Dé Danann / Aos Sí ('fairy folk') and the dead, with a courtly aristocratic culture; strict taboos govern contact — above all, never eat the food of the Otherworld or you can never return.

> **Key takeaway:** The deep European root of the 'parallel world next door': a realm woven into the real landscape, with the time-distortion and food-taboo motifs that fantasy still reuses today.

### The Norse Nine Worlds (Yggdrasil)

*Norse mythology*

> **Illustration (public domain):** Yggdrasil, the world-tree connecting the Nine Worlds (19th-century illustration). Public domain.

- **What makes it different —** Reality is structured as nine distinct worlds held together by the world-tree Yggdrasil: Asgard of the gods, Midgard of humans, Jötunheim of the giants, Álfheim of the elves, Helheim of the dead, and others. The worlds are connected (the rainbow bridge Bifröst links Asgard and Midgard) and travel between them is possible for gods and the dead.
- **Historical links —** Pre-Christian Scandinavian and Germanic belief, recorded in the medieval Icelandic Eddas. Uniquely, the Norse tried to map the worlds' relative positions rather than merely list them.
- **Geographical links —** Loosely tied to the northern landscape and seafaring culture; the cosmology mirrors a vertical world-axis (tree, mountains, rivers) common to Indo-European myth.
- **Anthropological character —** Distinct peoples per world — gods (Æsir and Vanir), giants, light- and dark-elves, dwarves, the dead — each with their own halls, politics, and feuds, and a warrior-honour culture centred on Valhalla.

> **Key takeaway:** An unusually systematic mythic multiverse: nine named, mapped worlds on a single cosmic tree, a clear ancestor of modern 'mapped' fantasy multiverses.

### Fairyland / Faerie

*British & European folk tradition*

- **What makes it different —** A hidden realm coextensive with our own, entered through fairy rings, hills, or mist. It runs on enchantment and on its own dangerous time: the classic motif of the mortal who dances one night with the fairies and returns to find a lifetime has passed.
- **Historical links —** Folk belief across the British Isles and Europe; later softened by literature (notably Shakespeare) from something genuinely feared into something whimsical.
- **Geographical links —** Tied to specific real features — particular hills, rings of mushrooms, ancient barrows — believed to be thresholds.
- **Anthropological character —** An aristocratic fairy court (a king and queen, a Seelie and Unseelie order in some traditions) with strict, perilous rules of hospitality, debt, and exchange.

> **Key takeaway:** The folk source for the 'beautiful but dangerous realm next door,' supplying the time-slip and the don't-eat-the-food rules that pervade later fiction.

## Part V — Theatre

The stage is the thinnest category for literal parallel universes, but it holds one of the most influential of all — Shakespeare's enchanted wood — and the symbolist dream-realms of the early twentieth century.

### The Athenian Wood

*Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night's Dream (c. 1595)*

> **Illustration (public domain):** Joseph Noel Paton, The Quarrel of Oberon and Titania (1849). Public domain.

- **What makes it different —** Outside the ordered city of Athens lies a moonlit forest ruled by the fairy king and queen, Oberon and Titania — a 'green world' where the daylight rules of law, hierarchy, and reason are suspended. Love is rerouted by magic flowers, identities blur, and a man is given a donkey's head. By dawn it can all be dismissed as a dream.
- **Historical links —** No historical events; the play fuses classical Athens (Theseus, Hippolyta) with English Midsummer folk tradition of the 1590s.
- **Geographical links —** A notional 'wood near Athens' that is really an idealised English forest — the wild green space outside the civilised town.
- **Anthropological character —** A fairy court drawn from English folklore (Puck/Robin Goodfellow, Oberon, Titania), reimagined by Shakespeare as benevolent and dreamlike rather than the feared spirits of older belief.

> **Key takeaway:** The 'green world' theatrical archetype: a parallel space just outside ordinary society where normal rules dissolve and transformation becomes possible — the dramatic ancestor of countless portal fantasies.

### The Land of Memory & the Future

*Maurice Maeterlinck, The Blue Bird (L'Oiseau bleu, 1908)*

- **What makes it different —** Two children searching for the Blue Bird of Happiness travel, in a dream-quest, through a series of allegorical otherworlds: the Land of Memory (where the dead grandparents 'wake' whenever remembered), the Palace of Night, the Kingdom of the Future (where unborn children wait), and a world where objects and elements have living souls.
- **Historical links —** No real history; an early-20th-century symbolist parable about happiness and the afterlife.
- **Geographical links —** Wholly allegorical, non-geographic spaces — Memory, Night, the Future.
- **Anthropological character —** Personified abstractions and souls — the dead who live as long as they are remembered, the not-yet-born, the spirits of Bread, Fire, Water, and the family Dog and Cat.

> **Key takeaway:** A theatrical multiverse of the inner life: each 'world' is a state of being (memory, death, the future) rather than a place — parallel realms as moral and emotional geography.

## Meta-Analysis: Patterns Across the Worlds

Surveyed together, these worlds share a small set of recurring structural devices — the same handful of ideas, invented independently by folk cultures and then reused by novelists, film-makers, and game designers.

| Recurring pattern | What it does, and where it shows up |
|---|---|
| **The threshold** | A specific crossing-point marks the boundary: a wardrobe (Narnia), a cyclone and colour-shift (Oz), a tunnel (Spirited Away), a fairy ring or burial mound (folklore), a cut "window" (His Dark Materials), a "tear" (BioShock). The threshold is emphasised more than the journey. |
| **Time runs differently** | Asynchronous time is near-universal in mythic and portal worlds: a night in Faerie or the Celtic Otherworld costs years at home; Narnian years pass in earthly minutes. |
| **The mirror / same map** | Many modern worlds keep the geography identical and change only the moral or physical "weather": the Upside Down, Zelda's Dark World, the four Londons, BioShock's tears. |
| **Rules and taboos** | The otherworld runs on strict, dangerous rules — don't eat the food (Faerie, Celtic Otherworld, Spirited Away), don't lose your name (Spirited Away), conquest only by tournament (Mortal Kombat). |
| **The uncanny double** | A real place doubled into something subtly wrong: Pullman's two Oxfords, the Spider-Verse's many New Yorks, Hill Valley's three versions. |
| **Style as reality** | Increasingly the difference is rendered aesthetically — a different art style per universe (Spider-Verse, Shattered Dimensions) or a colour-shift (Oz). |

**History:** strongest in games and literary alternate-histories (BioShock's American ideologies; His Dark Materials' altered Reformation; Oz and Back to the Future as political allegory). Mythic otherworlds are tied to ritual and ancestor-belief, not dated events.

**Geography:** real places matter most as *departure points* and as *doubles* — Oz leaves from Kansas; Stranger Things doubles Hawkins; Pullman doubles Oxford; the Spider-Verse multiplies New York. Folklore is the most physically grounded (real hills, lakes, islands such as Bull Rock off Ireland).

**Anthropology:** richest invented societies in games and epic fiction (BioShock's ideological cities, the Norse Nine Worlds, Pullman's bears and witches); the mirror-worlds (Upside Down, Distortion World) are deliberately post-human and empty — the horror is the *absence* of culture.

## Glossary of Terms

- **Alternate history** — A story set in a world where a real historical event went differently, branching the timeline away from our own.
- **Daemon (dæmon)** — In Philip Pullman's world, a person's soul living outside the body as a visible animal companion.
- **Faerie / Fairyland** — The folk otherworld of the fairies, coextensive with our own and famous for its dangerous, time-distorting enchantment.
- **Isekai** — A Japanese genre ("another world") in which an ordinary person is transported to or trapped in a parallel/fantasy world.
- **Many-worlds** — The idea, borrowed from a reading of quantum physics, that every possible outcome of every choice spawns its own branching universe.
- **Mirror world** — A parallel reality occupying the same physical space as ours but altered — darkened, decayed, or reversed.
- **Multiverse** — The set of all parallel universes considered together; a single reality within it is a "universe" or "world."
- **Otherworld** — The scholarly term for a mythic supernatural realm — of gods, the dead, or fairy-folk — existing beside the ordinary world.
- **Portal fantasy** — A story in which characters cross a threshold (door, wardrobe, tunnel) from the ordinary world into a secondary world.
- **Secondary world** — A fully invented, self-contained world with its own geography, history, and rules (Narnia, Oz, Mid-World).
- **Sídhe** — In Irish tradition, both the fairy-folk and the ancient burial mounds through which their Otherworld is entered.
- **Threshold** — The crossing-point between worlds; the device (and moment) most emphasised in parallel-world stories.
- **Tír na nÓg** — "The Land of Youth" — the best-known region of the Irish Otherworld, reached across the western sea.
- **Yggdrasil** — The cosmic world-tree of Norse myth that connects and supports the Nine Worlds.
